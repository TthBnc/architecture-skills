# Feedback rubric

Use this rubric to guide practice, not as a claim about a particular employer's hiring standard.

Assess what the conversation demonstrated:

| Dimension | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Problem framing | Important behavior prioritized; quality goals clarified; assumptions surfaced |
| Working design | Candidate can trace an end-to-end request or data flow with relevant state and interfaces |
| Correctness | Mechanisms support stated invariants, including relevant races or repeated effects |
| Tradeoffs | Choices tied to requirements; alternatives and limitations understood |
| Scaling and reliability | A consequential bottleneck or failure explored with useful estimates where needed |
| Communication | Reasoning is understandable, questions answered, feedback incorporated |

Feedback should distinguish independent reasoning, a response after a neutral probe, and an answer after a substantive hint. All can be useful learning, but they are different evidence. Quote short excerpts or paraphrase specific turns accurately. List unassessed dimensions separately from demonstrated gaps.

For an early-career practice session, favor completing a coherent baseline and understanding its parts. For a senior session, probe chosen tradeoffs and the main failure boundaries. For staff-level practice, invite the candidate to identify the highest-consequence ambiguity and lead the relevant investigation. These are adjustable exercise expectations, not validated universal hiring criteria.

A concise feedback form:

- What worked, supported by actual responses.
- One or two material gaps, with the relevant answer or missed probe.
- A next exercise that targets each gap.
- What the session did not assess and what help was supplied.

If the candidate gives only an opening response and stops, feedback must stay limited to that response. Avoid numeric scores unless requested. If scores are requested, explain the scale and uncertainty; never translate them into hiring probability or industry percentile.
